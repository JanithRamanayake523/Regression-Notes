9  Simple linear regression

Simple linear regression (SLR) — one covariate — is a special case of the general MLR machinery from the last three sections. Because it has only two coefficients, everything simplifies into closed-form scalar expressions, which is worth seeing explicitly: it connects the abstract matrix formula \(\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top Y\) back to the concrete slope/intercept formulas you may have already seen, and it reveals a direct link between the regression slope and the correlation coefficient from Unit_1_1.

Recall the SLR model \(Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+\epsilon_i\), with \[ y=\begin{pmatrix}y_1\\\vdots\\y_n\end{pmatrix},\quad X=\begin{pmatrix}1&x_1\\\vdots&\vdots\\1&x_n\end{pmatrix},\quad \beta=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_0\\\beta_1\end{pmatrix},\quad \epsilon=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_1\\\vdots\\\epsilon_n\end{pmatrix}. \]

9.1 Estimated coefficients in closed form

Direct matrix multiplication gives \[ X^\top X=\begin{pmatrix}n&\sum_i x_i\\\sum_i x_i&\sum_i x_i^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad X^\top y=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_i y_i\\\sum_i x_iy_i\end{pmatrix}. \] Using the \(2\times2\) inverse formula (\(\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}\)) and the identities \(\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)=\sum_ix_iy_i-n\bar x\bar y\) and \(\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2=\sum_ix_i^2-n\bar x^2\) from introductory statistics, \[ (X^\top X)^{-1}=\frac{1}{n\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}\begin{pmatrix}\sum_ix_i^2&-\sum_ix_i\\-\sum_ix_i&n\end{pmatrix}. \] Multiplying through by \(X^\top y\) and simplifying (Exercise 3.20 below) gives the familiar slope and intercept formulas:

Exercise 3.20 Show that \[ \hat\beta_1=\frac{n\sum_ix_iy_i-\sum_ix_i\sum_iy_i}{n\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}. \]

Does this look familiar? It’s exactly \[ \hat\beta_1=\hat{{\textrm{corr}}}(X,Y)\,\frac{\hat\sigma_y}{\hat\sigma_x}, \] where \(\hat{{\textrm{corr}}}(X,Y)\) is the sample correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\) (from Unit_1_1).

TipIntuition: the slope is correlation, rescaled

This identity says the estimated slope is entirely determined by two things: how strongly \(X\) and \(Y\) move together (their correlation) and how their individual spreads compare (the ratio \(\hat\sigma_y/\hat\sigma_x\)). If \(X\) and \(Y\) are essentially uncorrelated, \(\hat\beta_1\approx0\), no matter how variable either one is on its own — a flat line is the least-squares best fit. The \(\hat\sigma_y/\hat\sigma_x\) factor is a units conversion: it rescales the (unit-free, always-between \(-1\)-and-\(1\)) correlation into the actual units of “change in \(Y\) per unit of \(X\).”

Similarly for the intercept:

Exercise 3.21 Show that \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\).

TipIntuition: the fitted line always passes through \((\bar x,\bar y)\)

Rearranging \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\) gives \(\bar y=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1\bar x\) — in words, the point \((\bar x,\bar y)\), the “average” data point, always lies exactly on the least squares line, regardless of the data. This gives a useful sanity check: if your fitted line doesn’t pass through the means of \(X\) and \(Y\), something has gone wrong in the calculation.

9.2 Inference in SLR

Since \({\textrm{Var}}[\hat\beta]=(X^\top X)^{-1}\sigma^2\), plugging in the \(2\times2\) inverse above gives closed-form expressions: \[ {\textrm{Var}}\left[\hat\beta_0\right]=\left[\frac{1}{n}+\frac{\bar x^2}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}\right]\sigma^2,\qquad {\textrm{Var}}\left[\hat\beta_1\right]=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2},\qquad {\textrm{cov}}(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1)=-\frac{\bar x}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}\sigma^2. \]

Everything from 3.3 — confidence intervals \(\hat\beta_i\pm t_{dfE,\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{{\textrm{Var}}}(\hat\beta_i)}\), and tests of \(H_0:\beta_i=\beta_i^0\) using \(\dfrac{\hat\beta_i-\beta_i^0}{\sqrt{\widehat{{\textrm{Var}}}(\hat\beta_i)}}\sim t_{dfE}\) — applies exactly as before, now for \(i\in\{0,1\}\), with these plugged-in variance formulas. Inference for the mean response and predictions, and the ANOVA table, all simplify the same way; notably, in SLR, \(R^2\) is exactly the square of the sample correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\).

9.3 Inference for the correlation coefficient

SLR also gives us a way to do inference directly on the correlation \(\rho={\textrm{corr}}[X,Y]\), since at the population level, \(\beta_1=\rho\,\sigma_y/\sigma_x\) — the exact population analogue of the sample identity above.

Because \(\sigma_y/\sigma_x>0\), testing \(H_0:\rho=0\) vs. \(H_1:\rho\neq0\) is equivalent to testing \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) vs. \(H_1:\beta_1\neq0\) — so we can just re-use the slope test from 3.3. Writing \(\hat\rho=\hat{{\textrm{corr}}}(X,Y)\), the test statistic works out to \[ \frac{\hat\beta_1}{\sqrt{\widehat{{\textrm{Var}}}(\hat\beta_1)}}=\frac{\hat\rho\sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-\hat\rho^2}}, \] with p-value from \(t_{dfE}\), exactly as before.

WarningCommon mistake: expecting the same simple trick for \(\rho\neq0\)

Testing \(H_0:\rho=0\) reduces neatly to the slope test only because \(\rho=0\iff\beta_1=0\). If instead we want to test \(H_0:\rho=\rho_0\) for some nonzero \(\rho_0\), this trick fails — the exact distribution of \(\hat\rho\) under such a null is difficult to work with directly. This is why Fisher’s transformation (below) exists as a completely separate tool, not a simple extension of the slope test.

Fisher’s transformation. For \(\rho\in(-1,1)\), define \(\theta=\frac12\log\dfrac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\), estimated by \(\hat\theta=\frac12\log\dfrac{1+\hat\rho}{1-\hat\rho}\). Remarkably, \(\hat\theta\) is approximately Normal with mean \(\theta\) and variance \(\frac{1}{n-3}\) — regardless of the underlying distribution of \(X,Y\) — which gives an approximate \((1-\alpha)100\%\) CI for \(\theta\): \[\hat\theta\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n-3}},\] which can then be converted back to a CI for \(\rho\) via the inverse transformation. A hypothesized \(\rho_0\) similarly maps to \(\theta_0=\frac12\log\frac{1+\rho_0}{1-\rho_0}\), giving a standard-Normal-based test for \(H_0:\rho=\rho_0\) against any nonzero \(\rho_0\).

TipIntuition: why bother transforming \(\rho\) at all?

\(\rho\) is squeezed into \((-1,1)\), and its sampling distribution becomes badly skewed as \(\hat\rho\) approaches \(\pm1\) (there’s much more “room” for \(\hat\rho\) to be too small than too large near the boundary) — so a Normal approximation applied directly to \(\hat\rho\) would be poor except when \(\rho\) is close to 0. Fisher’s transformation stretches \((-1,1)\) out onto the whole real line, un-squeezing the distribution near the boundaries, so that the transformed quantity \(\hat\theta\) behaves (approximately) Normal everywhere.

Example 3.10 In Example 3.1, test whether the correlation between weight and body fat is 0, then test whether it’s greater than \(1/2\), and construct a 95% CI for \(\rho\).

cor(df)[1,2]        # sample correlation
[1] 0.6966328
summary(model)       # slope t-test IS the rho=0 test
Coefficients:
             Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|)
(Intercept) -27.37626   11.54743  -2.371 0.029119 *
Weight        0.24987    0.06065   4.120 0.000643 ***

Since \(H_0:\rho=0\) is equivalent to \(H_0:\beta_1=0\), the Weight row’s \(t\)-value (\(4.120\)) and p-value (\(0.0006\)) directly answer the first question: strong evidence that weight and body fat are correlated.

cor(df)[1,2]^2        # R^2 equals sample correlation squared
[1] 0.4852972

This matches Multiple R-squared: 0.4853 from summary(model) exactly, confirming the SLR identity \(R^2=\hat\rho^2\).

For \(H_0:\rho\leq1/2\) vs. \(H_1:\rho>1/2\), and for a 95% CI for \(\rho\), we’d apply Fisher’s transformation with \(\hat\rho=0.6966\), giving \(\hat\theta=\frac12\log\frac{1.6966}{0.3034}\approx0.858\); comparing against \(\theta_0=\frac12\log\frac{1.5}{0.5}\approx0.549\) (for \(\rho_0=1/2\)) via a one-sided Normal test, or building \(\hat\theta\pm z_{0.025}\sqrt{1/17}\) and inverse-transforming both endpoints for the CI.

NoteKey takeaways before moving on
  • SLR is just MLR with \(p=2\); the general matrix formulas collapse into closed-form scalar expressions for \(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1\) and their variances.
  • \(\hat\beta_1=\hat{{\textrm{corr}}}(X,Y)\cdot\hat\sigma_y/\hat\sigma_x\): the slope is the correlation, rescaled into the units of \(Y\) per unit of \(X\). The fitted line always passes through \((\bar x,\bar y)\).
  • In SLR, \(R^2\) is exactly the squared sample correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\).
  • Because \(\beta_1=\rho\,\sigma_y/\sigma_x\), testing \(H_0:\rho=0\) is exactly the same test as \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) — no new machinery needed.
  • Testing or building intervals for a nonzero \(\rho_0\) needs Fisher’s transformation, since \(\hat\rho\)’s own sampling distribution is not well-approximated by a Normal, especially near \(\pm1\).

9.4 Check Your Understanding

Before checking your answers, complete Exercises 3.20–3.22: derive \(\hat\beta_1=\dfrac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}\), show \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\), and — using the hours-studied/exam-score data from 3.2 — compute \(\hat\beta\), \({\textrm{Var}}[\hat\beta_1]\), \({\textrm{Var}}[\hat\beta_0]\), and \({\textrm{cov}}(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1)\) directly from the closed-form formulas, then confirm they match lm()’s output.

Question 1. If \(X\) and \(Y\) have sample correlation \(\hat\rho=0\), what must \(\hat\beta_1\) be, and why?

Show answer

\(\hat\beta_1=0\), since \(\hat\beta_1=\hat\rho\cdot\hat\sigma_y/\hat\sigma_x\) and multiplying by \(\hat\rho=0\) gives 0 regardless of the standard deviations. Zero linear correlation and a flat (zero-slope) least squares line are the same statement.

Question 2. Why does the least squares line in SLR always pass through the point \((\bar x,\bar y)\)?

Show answer

Because \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\), rearranging gives \(\bar y=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1\bar x\) — plugging \(x=\bar x\) into the fitted line always returns \(\bar y\) exactly, by construction of the least squares intercept formula.

Question 3. In SLR, how is \(R^2\) related to the sample correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\)?

Show answer

\(R^2=\hat\rho^2\) — the coefficient of determination is exactly the square of the sample correlation coefficient (this is specific to SLR with a single covariate; it doesn’t generalize the same way once there are multiple covariates).

Question 4. Why is testing \(H_0:\rho=0\) equivalent to testing \(H_0:\beta_1=0\), but testing \(H_0:\rho=0.4\) is not equivalent to testing \(H_0:\beta_1=0.4\sigma_y/\sigma_x\) in the same simple way?

Show answer

Both reduce to the same test only when the hypothesized value is 0: \(\rho=0\iff\beta_1=0\) exactly, via \(\beta_1=\rho\sigma_y/\sigma_x\). For a nonzero hypothesized \(\rho_0\), the exact sampling distribution of \(\hat\rho\) (or the corresponding \(\hat\beta_1\)) under that null is complicated and not a simple \(t\)-distribution, which is exactly why Fisher’s transformation is needed as a separate, approximate method.

Question 5. What does Fisher’s transformation \(\theta=\frac12\log\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\) accomplish, intuitively?

Show answer

It maps \(\rho\in(-1,1)\) onto the whole real line, “stretching out” the region near \(\pm1\) where \(\hat\rho\)’s sampling distribution is most skewed. The transformed estimator \(\hat\theta\) is then approximately Normally distributed (with a simple variance \(1/(n-3)\)) even when \(\hat\rho\) itself is not well-approximated by a Normal distribution.

Question 6. True or False: the formulas in this section (for \(\hat\beta\), its variance, etc.) require new assumptions beyond the general MLR model from earlier sections.

Show answer

False. SLR is just the \(p=2\) special case of MLR — the formulas here are the same general MLR results (\(\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top Y\), \({\textrm{cov}}(\hat\beta)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\), etc.), just simplified algebraically for the specific structure of a single-covariate \(X\) matrix. No new assumptions are introduced.
